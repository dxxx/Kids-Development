# Research: which games hook 5 to 7 year olds and actually build thinking skills

Goal: more mini-game ideas that keep a 5 year old coming back and build logic,
maths, reading and general thinking skills (executive functions, spatial
reasoning, planning), without manipulative design.

How this was produced: a multi-agent research run (59 agents, 24 sources, 9
claims extracted, 8 confirmed by 3-vote adversarial verification, 1 refuted),
then targeted searches to fill the gaps it left. The run could only read
abstracts because full texts were blocked, so its verified core is narrow.
Evidence strength is marked on every point below.

---

## 1. The single most important finding

**Practise the skill you want, directly.** Thinking-skill games do improve
thinking skills, but the gains stay there. They do not spill over into maths
or reading.

- A meta-analysis of executive-function training in children aged 3 to 6
  (32 studies) found a small to moderate gain on executive-function tasks,
  Hedges' g = 0.35. Gains transferred to other executive-function tasks but
  showed **no additional effect on behaviour or academic outcomes**.
  Delivery on a tablet or computer neither helped nor hurt.
  *(Scionti et al. 2020, Frontiers in Psychology, with 2020 corrigendum.
  Verified 3-0. Strength: high.)*
- Gains were larger for children at risk (ADHD, low income) than for typically
  developing children. Autism was not studied. For a child already ahead,
  expect smaller gains. *(Same source. Verified 3-0. Strength: medium.)*
- Diamond and Lee's review in Science (2011) found the same pattern across
  computer training, games, martial arts and school curricula: what works is
  **repeated practice with steadily increasing challenge**, and the weakest
  children gain most. *(Strength: high, widely cited review.)*

Design rule: maths games must contain maths, reading games must contain
reading. Logic and memory games are worth having for their own sake and for
variety, not as a back door to maths.

## 2. What the evidence supports, skill by skill

### Number sense: the linear number road (strongest evidence of anything here)
- About an hour of playing a simple **linear** number board game (squares
  numbered in a straight line) improved preschoolers' number-line estimation,
  magnitude comparison, counting and numeral recognition. Circular boards did
  not work; the straight line is the active ingredient.
  *(Siegler and Ramani 2008, 2009; Ramani and Siegler 2011 is a randomised
  trial that meets What Works Clearinghouse standards without reservations.
  Strength: high.)*
- With a 1 to 100 board, children who **counted on from where their piece
  stood** ("34, 35, 36") learned far more than children who counted from 1
  each time, and practising numerals 1 to 100 outside the board did not give
  the same gain. *(Laski and Siegler 2013. Strength: medium to high.)*
- Accuracy on 0 to 100 number lines predicts later maths reasoning in
  kindergarten. *(Correlational. Strength: medium.)*

### Spatial reasoning: rotation, assembly, building
- Spatial skills are trainable: average gain g = 0.47 across 217 studies,
  stable over time and transferring to untrained spatial tasks.
  *(Uttal et al. 2013, Psychological Bulletin. Strength: high.)*
- Spatial training also improves maths, g = 0.28 across 29 studies, with
  bigger effects when children use **physical manipulatives**. Digital-only
  training was weaker. *(Hawes, Gilligan-Lee and Mix 2022, Developmental
  Psychology. Strength: high for the effect, medium for the digital caveat.)*

Implication: tablet spatial puzzles are worth doing, and pairing them with a
real-world "build it with your blocks" prompt for the parent would add value.

### Coding and planning: sequencing a car through a map
- A 2024 meta-analysis of coding with children aged 4 to 16 found the largest
  gains in **problem solving (d = 0.89)** and **planning (d = 0.36)**, small
  gains in inhibition (0.17) and working memory (0.20). Structured virtual or
  tangible coding worked for preschool and first grade; open-ended Scratch-style
  coding worked only for older children.
  *(Montuori et al. 2024, Computers and Education. Strength: medium; not
  independently verified by the run.)*
- A small UK trial with 5 to 6 year olds found short programming lessons no
  better than plain arithmetic practice for maths or spatial scores, and no
  working-memory gain. *(Messer et al. 2018. Verified 3-0. Strength: medium,
  small sample.)*

Implication: structured "program the car" puzzles are well justified as
planning and problem-solving practice. Do not count them as maths.

### Executive functions: memory, stop and go, rule switching
Covered in section 1: real but modest gains, limited to those skills, best
with steadily increasing challenge. Good as short, varied rounds.

### Reading comprehension
The run found nothing new. The curriculum research already in this repo
(`RESEARCH_UK_YEAR1_YEAR2.md`) gives the five comprehension strands to
practise directly: vocabulary, retrieval, sequencing, inference, prediction.

### Realistic photos
Toddlers carry what they learn from picture books into the real world most
easily from photographs and least easily from cartoons. That effect weakens
as children's symbolic understanding grows, so at 5 it is a small advantage.
*(Ganea, Pickard and DeLoache 2008, as summarised in Strouse, Nyhout and Ganea
2018. Strength: medium for toddlers, low for age 5.)*

## 3. What makes an app good for learning, and what to avoid

### The four pillars
A review in Psychological Science in the Public Interest set out four
features of apps that teach: children are **actively** thinking ("minds-on",
not just tapping), **engaged** without distraction, the content is
**meaningful** and connected to their life, and there is **social
interaction** around it. Self-pacing matters.
*(Hirsh-Pasek et al. 2015. Strength: high as a framework.)*

### Manipulative design to avoid
In the apps that children aged 3 to 5 used most, only 20% had **no**
manipulative design. The common tricks were:
- lures to return ("Come back tomorrow and get a dragon")
- fabricated time pressure
- characters pressuring the child ("Don't just stand there, do something!")
- navigation that makes it hard to stop
- adverts and purchase prompts
*(Radesky et al. 2022, JAMA Network Open. Strength: high for prevalence.)*

**Rules for this game**, most already in place:
- no daily rewards, streaks, countdowns or "come back tomorrow" lures
- no timers that punish; no idle nagging
- characters never guilt the child for leaving or losing
- the leave button always works, with no penalty
- no ads, purchases or links out
- rewards are things he builds and keeps (crew, map, garage), not random drops

### Hooks that are not manipulative
These come from the design principles above and the engagement research
already in this repo, not from new verified studies:
- **collection with meaning**: a real-photo field guide of the crew with one
  true fact per animal, in three languages
- **progress you can see**: the car moving across a real map of the world
- **mastery**: puzzles that get harder exactly as he gets better
- **choice**: which route, which crew member drives, which car
- **curiosity**: a closed box at each stop that opens when the trip ends
- **shared play**: one suggestion for the parent after each session

## 4. Ranked mini-game ideas

Ranked by evidence for the skill, fit with this child (fluent reader, strong
counter, loves cars and animals, prefers games to being questioned), and
engagement value. All keep the job-or-question forms, hint fading and no-fail
rules from `GAME_DESIGN.md`.

| Rank | Game | What he does | Skill built | Evidence | Engagement hook |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Number Road Rally** | A straight road numbered 0 to 100. Spin, then drive the car forward, tapping each square while counting on from where it stands. Later: "Park at 63", find it on an unmarked road. | Number line, counting on, magnitude | High (Siegler and Ramani, randomised trials) | Racing a crew member; real road photos; the car visibly moves |
| 2 | **Program the Car** | Lay arrow tiles to drive the car through a city map to the garage. Later: repeat blocks (loops), and fix a route someone else got wrong (debugging). | Planning, problem solving, sequencing | Medium (coding meta-analysis) | Watching his program run; the "aha" of fixing a bug |
| 3 | **Shape Garage** | Rotate and fit parts into the outline of a car or truck; tangram vehicles; mirror the other half. Parent prompt: build it with real blocks. | Spatial reasoning, mental rotation | High for spatial, medium for maths transfer | Building a vehicle that then drives off |
| 4 | **Story Road** | Read a 3 to 6 sentence story about the crew at this stop; act on it: put events in order, pick what happens next, find the clue that tells you how the lemur feels. | Reading comprehension strands 1a to 1e | Direct practice of curriculum skills | The crew's adventures continue between trips |
| 5 | **Fair Share Market** | Share bananas, fish or tires equally between crew members; then halves, quarters and thirds of a pizza or a road. | Division, fractions (Year 1 and 2) | Direct curriculum practice | Feeding the crew; real food photos |
| 6 | **Balance Bridge** | A bridge balances when both sides weigh the same: put animals or crates on each side, find the missing weight. | Equality, missing numbers, early algebra | Low direct evidence, strong curriculum fit | Satisfying tip and level; heavy hippo jokes |
| 7 | **Convoy Memory** | A line of vehicles drives into a tunnel; rebuild the order when they come out. Later: backwards order, or one vehicle swapped. | Working memory | Medium for memory only | Short, quick, very visual |
| 8 | **Traffic Lights** | Tap the cars on green, hold on red; then the rule switches ("now tap the animals, not the cars"). | Inhibition, flexible thinking | Medium for these skills only | Fast and physical; good palate cleanser |
| keep | Load the Truck | Already built | Place value, counting on | Direct practice | Upgrade with photos and a drive-off |
| keep | Number Riddles | Already built | Number reasoning, reading | Direct practice | Recast as a detective lemur hunt |
| keep | Pattern Road | Already built | Patterns, skip counting | Direct practice | Merge into Number Road Rally later |
| keep | Parrot Words | Already built | Three languages | Direct practice | Real photos for every word |

### Recommended build order
1. **Number Road Rally**: strongest evidence, uses his love of counting high,
   and the road becomes the visual spine of the whole game.
2. **Program the Car**: biggest jump in logic and planning, and the most
   "game-like" for a car fan.
3. **Story Road**: comprehension is the known risk for very early readers.
4. **Shape Garage**: spatial skills are the one area not covered at all yet.
5. Fair Share Market, Balance Bridge, Convoy Memory, Traffic Lights.

## 5. Open questions the research could not answer

- Product-level trials of DragonBox, Lightbot, ScratchJr, Thinkrolls and
  similar: the run could not verify any.
- Experimental comparisons of engagement mechanics (collection, narrative,
  character attachment) for this age without manipulative rewards.
- How any of this changes for a highly able, trilingual child with possible
  autistic traits. Watching his play in the grown-ups corner is the best
  evidence available.
