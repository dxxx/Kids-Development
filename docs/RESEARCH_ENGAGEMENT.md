# Research: keeping kids engaged in learning

Focus: a 5 year old who reads since age 2, speaks three languages, counts to
500, follows instructions well in games but does not like answering questions
from people, and may have autistic traits.

This is a summary of published research and practitioner guidance, with notes
on how strong each source is and what it means for the game. It is not a
diagnosis and not medical advice.

---

## 1. What the profile suggests

Reading very early (age 2) with strong letter and number skills is a pattern
called hyperlexia. Researchers at McGill describe these children as following
their own unique pathway to literacy: word reading is a real strength, while
understanding what was read often lags behind. A small 2021 study of
preschoolers with autism and hyperlexia found that a parent-supported,
tablet-based program improved reading comprehension, and gains were in
receptive language (understanding) more than expressive language (speaking).

Not answering questions from people, while happily following instructions in a
game, fits two things the research describes:

- **Questions feel like demands.** A direct question with a right or wrong
  answer is a social demand with a person waiting. Practitioner guidance on
  demand avoidance says this is usually anxiety, not defiance, and that
  pressure, rewards and consequences tend to make it worse. A 2018 letter in
  The Lancet Child and Adolescent Health makes the same point: avoidance in
  autism is often anxiety-driven and standard behaviour management can
  escalate it.
- **Games remove the person.** Interviews with autistic adolescents about
  gaming found the main themes were agency ("I can do it without someone
  watching over me and giving constant instruction"), belonging, and emotion
  regulation. Nobody is waiting, nobody is judging, and the pace is theirs.

So the game is not working around a weakness. It is using the exact setting
where he is already comfortable.

**One honest note.** Early hyperlexia plus reluctance to answer people is a
pattern where a speech-language pathologist or developmental paediatrician
assessment is often recommended, mainly to check whether comprehension and
expressive language are keeping up with his reading. That is worth doing
regardless of the game. The game can support, it cannot replace it.

## 2. What the research says about engagement

### 2.1 Autonomy is the engine

Self-determination theory (Ryan and Deci) says motivation stays intrinsic when
three needs are met: autonomy (I chose this), competence (I can do this), and
relatedness (I belong). Decades of research back this across ages and
cultures. For young children the key form of autonomy is choice, even small
choice: which game, which car, which order.

For a child who resists demands, autonomy is not a nice to have. It is the
difference between playing and shutting down.

### 2.2 Predictability and clear rules

Multiple reviews of educational games for autistic children report that
predictable structure, clear goals and immediate feedback are what works.
Children on the spectrum often prefer systems with explicit, consistent rules.
UI reviews add: same things in the same places, no surprise rearrangements,
no sudden sounds or flashes, calm palette, and user control over sound and
motion. A 2026 systematic review called visual simplicity and sensory-friendly
design "foundational", while noting more testing is needed.

### 2.3 Special interests pull attention

Using a child's interests (cars, animals, favourite characters) in learning
material is widely recommended and low risk. It reliably helps engagement and
time on task. It is not proven to raise achievement by itself, so the task
design still has to be good; the interest gets the child to the task.

### 2.4 Errors: allow them, but do not punish them

A 2020 randomised trial with 28 autistic participants compared errorless
learning (steer toward the right answer, prevent mistakes) with error
correction (let mistakes happen, then correct). Both worked, neither caused
distress. Errorless produced fewer mistakes during teaching. Practitioner
reports from parents say children do best when wrong answers are simply not
accepted (the piece does not fit) rather than marked wrong.

Design takeaway: let the child try, never flash a red X, and when stuck, fade
in a prompt that makes the right move obvious. Camp Discovery, an autism
learning app, does exactly this: prompts appear on a new skill and fade as the
child succeeds.

### 2.5 Teaching without asking: two proven models

**DragonBox** teaches algebra to 5 year olds by starting as a puzzle: move
cards to isolate a box. The rules are discovered by experimenting, not
explained. Symbols replace pictures only later. The child never answers a
question; they solve a puzzle whose rules happen to be algebra.

**Toca Boca** makes "digital toys", not games: no rules, no scores, no winning
or losing, no instructions. "A good toy doesn't need instructions." Children
learn (how food changes when cooked, how a city works) by doing, not by being
asked.

Both are used by millions of children and neither relies on question and
answer. For this child, that is the core lesson.

### 2.6 Declarative language instead of questions

Practitioner guidance for children who resist questions recommends replacing
questions with observations and "I wonder" statements, offering a choice of
two instead of an open question, giving processing time, and keeping language
concrete and literal. These are human-to-human strategies, but the same
wording principles apply to a game's voice.

## 3. What this means for the game

### 3.1 Questions are fine when he is engaged, jobs are safer when he is not

His parent reports that inside an engaging game he answers questions without
trouble. The pressure comes from a person waiting, not from the question
itself. So the game does not need to avoid questions. It needs to keep
engagement high and use the right form at the right moment:

- **Jobs for anything new or hard.** When a skill is introduced or the level
  goes up, present it as a job with a visible goal: "The truck needs 8
  tires." The math is inside the job and there is nothing to get wrong out
  loud.
- **Questions once he is rolling.** When he is on a run of successes,
  direct questions are fine and are good practice: "How many tires are on
  the truck now?" with tappable answers.
- **Drop back to jobs when engagement dips.** If he leaves a game early or
  misses twice, the next round of that skill is a job, not a question.

| Job form | Question form (when engaged) |
|---|---|
| "One animal got on the wrong bus. Move it." | "Which animal does not belong?" |
| "Finish the road so the car can drive." | "What comes next?" |
| "Park the cars that are still here." | "How many are left?" |
| "Give the sign to the animal who can read it." | "Which language is this?" |

Both forms exist for every mini-game. The trip engine chooses between them
from recent engagement, not at random.

### 3.2 No one is waiting

- No timers, no "hurry", no idle prompts that nag ("Are you still there?").
- The crew reacts to what he does, never asks him to do something again.
- Silence is fine. If he does nothing for a minute, nothing happens.

### 3.3 Wrong is just "not yet"

- A wrong piece does not snap in. It slides back. No sound of failure.
- After two misses, a hint fades in: the right spot glows, or a crew member
  demonstrates once.
- After three misses the job simplifies (fewer options) rather than ending.
- No visible score, streak, or "3 out of 5".

### 3.4 Choice everywhere

- He picks the next stop on the map (two or three options, all valid).
- He picks which crew member rides along, which car, which colour.
- He can leave a mini-game by tapping the car, no penalty. The game simply
  offers a different job next time.

### 3.5 Predictable, calm, consistent

- Same layout in every mini-game: scene in the middle, car button bottom
  left, the job shown as a picture plus one short line at the top.
- Sounds are soft, no sudden effects. Sound and animation can be reduced in
  the parent corner.
- The crew behaves the same every time. Silly, but predictable.

### 3.6 Discovery first, words later

Like DragonBox: introduce a new idea as a puzzle he can solve by trying.
Name it later. Example: fair sharing is played for days as "every lemur gets
the same" before the word "divide" or the symbol appears, and the symbol is
shown, never asked about.

### 3.7 Interests as the surface, not the lesson

Cars and animals are in every scene. The lesson underneath is logic or math.
Rotate scenes so no single theme wears out.

### 3.8 Using the game to help the human side

Because he answers the game but not people, the game can build a bridge
without pressure:

- **Parent play prompts.** The parent corner suggests one thing to do
  together after a session, phrased as a shared job, not a question. "Ask
  him to show you his garage" is a demand. "Look at his garage together and
  say which car you like" is not.
- **Narrating, not asking.** The crew models declarative talk: "I see three
  red cars." Over time, offer a tap that lets him "say" something back
  (choose a line), which is a low-pressure way to practise answering.
- **No celebration that needs a reaction.** The crew celebrates among
  themselves. He is never asked "Did you like that?".

### 3.9 Comprehension, not decoding

Since reading is strong and comprehension is the known risk area for early
readers, the reading track should be entirely comprehension: follow written
instructions, act on a sentence, order a story, pick the picture that matches
the text. Never letter or sound drills.

## 4. Things to watch when he plays

The parent corner should surface these, because they tell you more than a
score:

- jobs he leaves early (which skill, which level)
- how long he stays after the hint appears
- whether he chooses the same mini-game every time (fine, but note it)
- whether session length stays steady or drops when difficulty rises
